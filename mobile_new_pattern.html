Eight destinations, five slots

A bottom bar physically holds four or five items. You have eight. Below are the four honest ways out, live on a phone frame — switch between them, tap the bars, open the sheets. My recommendation and the code changes are at the bottom.

Why five, and not eight

A touch target must be at least 44 × 44px, and WCAG 2.2 requires the layout to stay usable down to 320px wide. Divide the bar and the answer is arithmetic:

8 tabs at 320px = 40px each — below the minimum. At 360px (most Android phones) = 45px, which technically passes but leaves no room for a legible label. “Wild products” becomes “Wild pr…”.

4 tabs at 320px = 80px each — comfortable target, full label, room for the thumb.

There is a second reason beyond pixels: people don't scan eight icons. They scan three or four and then stop. Eight equal-weight tabs means every destination is equally unimportant.

What I'd actually ship

Pattern B, with A as the safety net

Consolidate to four real destinations, and keep a “More” entry inside the fourth for the long tail. You get comfortable targets, readable labels, and — the important part — a mobile IA that finally tells the vendor what matters instead of presenting eight equal choices.

8 → 4, and nothing was deleted. Every screen is still one or two taps away. What changed is that the phone now says “these are the four things you do here”, which is true, instead of “here are eight equally weighted options”, which was never true.

Rules for whichever pattern you pick

  • Minimum 48px touch height on the bar, 44px absolute floor. Add env(safe-area-inset-bottom) padding for the iPhone home indicator.
  • Labels always on. Icon-only bottom bars fail badly for infrequent users, and half of your vendors open the app twice a week. If a label doesn't fit, you have too many tabs — that is the signal, not a font-size problem.
  • Never more than five slots, and the fifth is “More”, never a real destination competing with the other four.
  • Active state uses the filled glyph plus the 3px accent edge, so it reads without colour — the greyscale test still applies.
  • The balance strip stays above the content on every screen. Vendors check affordability constantly; the bar is not where that lives.
  • Badge the tab, not the app: an unlocked trade puts a dot on Wallet, a rejected purchase puts one on History.
  • Never put Log out in a bottom bar. It is one mis-tap from a destination and it costs the vendor their session mid-purchase.
  • Never hide the bar on scroll. Reappearance is unpredictable and it breaks re-orientation.

// nav.config.js
export const DESTINATIONS = [ /* all 8, with icon, path, label */ ];
export const TABS_MOBILE  = ['dashboard','buy','wallet','history'];
export const MORE_MOBILE  = ['claims','referrals','profile','logout'];

One config, two renderers. The rail keeps all eight on desktop; the bar reads TABS_MOBILE. Add a destination later and you edit one array, not two components. Render the bar below md only — useBreakpointValue({ base: true, md: false }) in Chakra.

If the client insists on all eight visible

Ship pattern A, not D. A “More” sheet with a 3-across grid shows all four remaining destinations at once, with 80px targets — arguably more visible than eight 40px tabs nobody can hit. Frame it that way and the objection usually dissolves.
